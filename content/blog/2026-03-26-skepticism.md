---
title: "Out of the Flames of Skepticism: Hume and Kant on Causality"
date: 2026-3-26
blurb: A piece that juxtaposes Hume and Kant's arguments about cause and effect. 
---
The question of causality is one that has long since plagued philosophers. From Aristotle’s four causes to the many philosophers who attribute God to be the first and only cause of everything, a wide range of perspectives on causation have developed over the centuries. In their schools of philosophy, David Hume and Immanuel Kant also take on this challenge. Specifically, in Hume’s Enquiries concerning Human Understanding and concerning the Principles of Morals, he examines the issue through a radically empiricist lens that begets a high degree of skepticism. In contrast, Kant’s perspective in A Treatise of Human Nature is marked by transcendental idealism, a framework notable for distinguishing between appearances and things-in-themselves, that allows him to move past Hume’s (almost debilitating) skepticism. While there are merits and faults to both perspectives, Kant’s critique of Hume is nevertheless successful. 

Hume begins his discussion of causation with a series of arguments against necessary connection because he cannot conceive a rational foundation for it. Firstly, he asserts that, while sensation gives us knowledge of how events appear to the “outward senses, the mind feels no sentiment or inward impression from this succession of objects” (Hume, 50). As illustrated by his billiard ball example, Hume means that, while the mind can perceive that “the impulse of one billiard ball is attended with motion in the second,” it cannot perceive any force or power that would make the second ball's motion necessary. After all, if the mind alone were able to perceive power or necessary connection between two events, it should be able to “foresee the effect, even without experience” (Hume, 50). However, in reality, this is clearly not the case: necessary connection between bodies cannot be derived from “single instances of their operation,” let alone entirely without experience (Hume, 50). 

Hume then turns inward, and examines both mind-body and mind-mind interactions in an attempt to discern some power or necessary connection that provides logical grounds for causation. This discussion, however, proves just as unfruitful because of his fundamental skepticism towards our understanding of the mind, body and soul’s relationship. He questions whether there is “any principle in all nature more mysterious than the union of soul with body,” and exemplifies his doubt through our lack of understanding about how the will actually affects motion in our limbs (Hume, 52). He substantiates this doubt through two related, but distinct inconsistencies: a) the will is able to affect voluntary action (e.g. movement of the tongue and fingers) but not autonomic functions (e.g. heart or liver function) and b) the will to move one’s limbs remains constant before and after a healthy man develops paralysis, yet in the latter case, he is unable to direct his limbs (Hume, 52). In both instances, it is clear that our understanding of the will and mind-body interaction is woefully incomplete. 

Moreover, our understanding of the mind’s interaction with itself is even more occluded. For example, we can neither understand how new ideas arise nor control our emotions as we do our ideas. These instances that elucidate the gaps in our understanding of the mind are foundational in Hume’s conclusion about the weakness of human reason. Building off that conclusion, he dismantles other philosophers’ argument for God as a universal cause because, “we have no idea of the Supreme Being but what we learn from reflection on our own faculties” and our faculties are faulty and unreliable (Hume, 57). Interestingly, though, the very weakness of human reason that dismantles rational accounts of causation is also what allows Hume to rehabilitate it: not as a rational inference, but as a foundational feature of the mind’s operation.

For Hume, causation becomes more of a human belief than true necessary connection grounded in rationality. By recategorizing causation as a psychological phenomenon rather than something that exists in nature, it becomes viable once more. Hume argues that observation only allows us to see sequences of events that “seem conjoined, but never connected” (Hume, 58). Going back to his example with the billiard balls, he claims that “the first time a man saw…the shock of two billiard balls, he could not pronounce that the one event was connected: but only that it was conjoined with the other” (Hume, 59). However, as we observe more and more instances of the second ball moving after the first ball is hit, the mind “is carried by habit, upon the appearance of one event, to expect its usual attendant, and to believe that it will exist” (Hume, 59). When we perceive similar events followed by the same effect with enough frequency, the brain attributes the preceding event to be the cause and the event that follows to be the effect. Thus, Hume believes that causation is not actually the existence of necessary connection, just constant conjunction and a belief or feeling that there is some necessary connection. 

Interestingly, Kant shares many of the same premises as Hume, but the conclusions he reaches actually serve as a critique of Humean empiricism and its reduction of causation to mere habit. To start, Kant agrees with Hume that we only perceive sequences of events and not necessary connection. However, his formulation, though similar to Hume’s, posits from the very beginning that “connection is not the work of mere sense and intuition, but is here rather the product of a synthetic faculty of the imagination” (Kant, B233). From the start, Kant argues that our experiences require active synthesis by the mind in order to create a temporal sequence. Indeed, the role of the mind’s structure in facilitating human experience is critical to his argument. 

Before he can conjecture on the nature of causality, though, Kant must clarify a key distinction: the difference between subjective and objective sequences. Subjective sequences are just an arbitrary order of events gleaned from our perception, while objective sequences are universally perceived as such. To illustrate the difference between the two categories, Kant uses the examples of a house and a ship. Starting with his house example, Kant claims that “apprehension of the manifold in the appearance of a house that stands before [him] is successive,” but the house itself is clearly not (Kant, B235). By this he means that a person (the subject) could perceive the house top-down (roof, second floor, first floor), bottom-up (first floor, second floor, roof), left-to-right or right-to-left and still perceive the same house (Kant, B238). The house is a static object, which means the order of perception depends solely on the viewer and can be reordered without undercutting the truth. All in all, one’s perception of a house is an example of a subjective sequence because the subject’s order of apprehension is arbitrary. 

In contrast, one’s perception of a ship moving downstream is an example of an objective sequence because the order of events is necessary and irreversible. The perception of the ship as being upstream and then downstream is fixed and cannot be reasonably conceived in any other order. As such, “the order in the sequence of the perceptions in apprehension is therefore here determined” and our perception of the sequence of events is bound to said determined order (Kant, B237). Therefore, the crucial distinction between a subjective sequence and an objective one is that, in the latter, the order of perceptions is fixed and necessary. 

Indeed, Kant believes that, without objective sequences, subjective sequences “would be entirely undetermined and no appearance would be distinguished from any other” (Kant, B238). While this initially seems at odds with his prior arguments, it actually reconciles quite nicely to create his concept of causality. For Kant, subjective sequences “[prove] nothing about the connection of the manifold in the object because [they are] entirely arbitrary” (Kant, B238). Subjective sequences can be rearranged at will and still allow the viewer to reach the same conclusion about the static object. In contrast, objective sequences cannot because “the order of the manifold of appearance…[is] in accordance with a rule” that renders the viewer unable to “arrange the apprehension otherwise than in exactly this sequence” (Kant, B238). For Kant, causality is this rule: the a priori principle that binds our perception of successive events into an objective, determined order.

Thus, for Kant, causality becomes a precursor, not a consequence, of experience. That is, causality is a necessary structure in our mind that enables us to experience. This is where Kant and Hume diverge the most as Hume clearly believed causality follows from experience. However, Kant successfully attacks his conclusion. He claims that while we only gain a clearer understanding of the rules of causality by “[making] use of [causality] in experience,” this nevertheless means that causality must have been “the ground of experience itself, and therefore preceded it a priori” (Kant, B241). While causality may only be recognized as a result of the experiences it induces, without causality existing first (a priori), experience would not be possible. 

However, while Kant’s critique of Hume is compelling, it is not without its faults. Although Hume’s account of causation could be seen as reductive, it nevertheless preserves a higher level of intellectual integrity by being grounded solely in what we can observe. Causation for Hume is merely habit, and he leaves room for causation to break down. The constant conjunction between events A and B carries no guarantee of necessity; it remains conceivable that the sequence could collapse entirely. This vulnerability is not a problem for Kant because causation is posited as a part of the mind’s a priori structure. Yet, in doing so, it almost feels like Kant did not so much resolve Hume’s worry as relocate it. Now, instead of the question being why we believe in a necessary connection that is not actually there, one could press Kant by asking why the a priori mental structure he defines actually mirrors reality. Kant himself acknowledges this limit, saying that we are only able to perceive representations and appearances of reality, never reality itself. While this caveat quietly undermines the efficacy of his critique, his arguments nevertheless largely maintain their philosophical force.  

Hume and Kant represent two coherent but irreconcilable responses to the question of causality. While they originate from similar premises, the two arrive at surprisingly contrasting conclusions about the nature of cause and effect. Hume, in being committed to grounding all knowledge in observation, fails to rationally justify causation and is forced to reframe it as a psychological phenomenon. Kant avoids this difficulty by elevating causation to being an inescapable, a priori structure of the mind that is a precondition for any, and all, experience. Although Kant’s critique of Hume is structurally compelling, it also has a weakness (causality is postulated as an indisputable part of the mind’s architecture) that diminishes, but does not destroy, the strength of his argument. Even though Kant’s perspective on causality has its faults, it nevertheless remains a satisfactory critique of Hume that rescues science and metaphysics from the flames of skepticism.
