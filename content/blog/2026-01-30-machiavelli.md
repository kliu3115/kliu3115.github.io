---
title: "Machiavelli’s Perfect Principality: The Interplay of Fortune and Virtue in Political Life"
date: 2026-01-30
blurb: A piece discussing how Machiavellian virtú reconciles with fortune. 
---
In Niccolo Machiavelli’s treatise The Prince, he lays the foundations of modern political science by setting out a series of pragmatic (and, at times, morally dubious) principles that leaders should follow to maintain power. In contrast to his classical and medieval predecessors, Machiavellian virtue is not moralistic or religious, but rather realistic: it focuses on how people actually act, rather than how they should act. Broadly speaking, Machiavellian virtue encompasses all of the qualities needed to maintain power in the face of fortune (e.g, cunning, strength, skill, etc.). Although Machiavelli’s view of fortune is complex and multifaceted, he generally views fortune negatively, even using it as an insidious warning to Lorenzo de Medici in his Dedicatory Letter. Despite his disdain for fortune, Machiavelli is forced to wrestle with its commanding presence in both his political philosophy and his own life and political exile. Fortune is an unpredictable force that cannot be controlled, but can be mitigated through exercising sufficient Machiavellian virtue; despite this, fortune remains necessary for acquiring political power, but insufficient for maintaining it. 

In Chapter 25 of The Prince, Machiavelli offers two powerful analogies for fortune: a river and a woman. Firstly, he characterizes fortune as a “torrential river…[that] directs her attacks where she knows banks and barriers have not been built to hold her” (P 25). From this account, it seems as though fortune deliberately chooses to target states, like Italy, that are unfortified and ill-equipped to resist her onrush. Yet, Machiavelli’s description of how similar circumstances would have impacted Germany, Spain, and France, which he claims had taken the proper precautions, complicates this image. Continuing the river analogy, he claims that, for the latter three states, “either the flood would not have had the consequences it had, or the banks would not even have been overwhelmed” (P 25). Regardless, Machiavelli implies that fortune’s path would not have changed. Rather, countries that had made preparations would have fared better because of their heightened ability to resist her detriment.

In his second analogy, he furthers this opinion by comparing fortune to a lady who can often be overcome by one’s boldness and mastered by being beaten and struck (P 25). One reading of this passage is that, through being headstrong, humans can actually make fortune submit to them. But, Machiavelli would likely disapprove of humans thinking they can control fortune. Indeed, from the very beginning in his Letter to Vettori, Machiavelli asserts that “fortune wants to control everything [and] evidently wants to be left a free hand,” unimpeded by human beings. He advises people not to get in her way, but, at the same time, maintains the existence of human agency because “fortune determines one half of our actions, but…leaves us to control the other half” (P 25). While these two claims appear contradictory, they can be somewhat reconciled with the fortune’s innate volatility. Interestingly, in both cases, fortune reigns supreme and has the power of choice: it can choose to either control everything or leave half to human decision. 

Machiavelli definitely believes in the latter, as The Prince is centered around advising leaders how to exercise their agency in order to maintain power. Thus, in his discussion of how to treat fortune as a lady, Machiavelli is not positing a universal law, but making an observation about how fortune historically tends to respond to certain modes of action. For example, Pope Julius II’s impetuous nature allowed him to conquer Bologna because his ferocity both scared the Venetians and capitalized on opportunistic conditions like Spain's weakness and France’s momentum. By being perpetually impetuous, Julius “achieved something no other pope…had been able to achieve” and never experienced failure (P 25). Empirically, Julius’ success substantiates the claim that boldness can generally overcome fortune. Yet, Machiavelli still views Julius pessimistically and believes that Julius simply did not live long enough for the times to change and destroy him. Despite his success, Julius was not considered an exemplar of Machiavellian virtue. 

Although virtú is translated into myriad words like strength and skill by the editor, David Wooton, Machiavellian virtue can be encapsulated as an antithesis to his concept of fortune. When Machiavelli begins delineating the different types of principalities, he draws an explicit distinction between “dominions that are acquired…through fortune or through strength [virtú]” and posits that “he who relies least on luck has the best prospect of success” (P 1, 6). He expands on this in Chapter 25, writing that “a ruler who depends entirely on his good fortune will be destroyed when his luck changes…[but] if one knew how to change one’s character as times and circumstances change, one’s luck would never change” (P 25). In line with the established understandings of fortune and virtue, Machiavelli maintains that fortune is ever-changing and uncontrollable. But, he also adds that, if people are virtuous and have the skill and foresight to adapt to the times, they will not be dependent on fortune. On the contrary, their virtue will enable them to oppose her and her destructive capabilities. 
This tension is, perhaps, best illustrated by Machiavelli’s armed prophets: Moses, Theseus, Cyrus, Romulus, and Hiero of Syracuse, among others. While the five range from religious to political to (comparatively) unremarkable and are an eclectic mix of figures, they are united by “their remarkable political skill [virtu] that enabled them to recognize” unfortunate circumstances as opportunities to ennoble their respective states (P 6). Their virtue lies in their unique ability to conceive revolutionary visions and execute them. Being armed was also critical for their success; it further bolstered their virtue by giving them the strength to enforce their new regimes and ideologies, unlike unarmed prophets such as Friar Girolamo Savonarola. As the pinnacles of Machiavellian virtue, the armed prophets were able to overcome terrible misfortunes (e.g., oppression of the Israelites by the Egyptians, exile from Alba, Athenian chaos, etc.) through their military strength and skillful insight. 

Based on Machiavelli’s exaltation of the armed prophets, one might conclude that he believes fortune should have no place in political life and that one should solely prioritize virtue. However, Machiavelli would certainly disagree. When describing the armed prophets, he highlights their dependency “on chance only for their first opportunity” and states that, “without that first opportunity, their strength [virtu] of purpose would never have been revealed” (P 6). Moreover, he uses words like “necessary” and “essential” when recounting the terrible conditions the states were in prior to the armed prophet's arrival. Thus, while fortune is insufficient for maintaining political power, it is necessary for creating the conditions that allow leaders to seize control and display their virtue. After the initial opportunity afforded by luck, though, rulers should not rely on fortune because it is too capricious. 

Nevertheless, Machiavelli’s account of Cesare Borgia’s political trajectory casts further doubt on his claim that fortune can be relied on at all, even initially. In The Prince, Machiavelli writes that he “cannot find anything to criticize” about Borgia and claims that there is no “better model to imitate than Cesare Borgia” in the recent past (P 7). During his time as Duke of Romagna, Borgia addressed immediate dangers by diminishing the formidable power of the Orsini and Colonna families and mitigated future threats of invasion by raising a strong and loyal military force. By Machiavelli’s standards, it seems absurd that someone so prescient and prudent would fall from grace, yet, following the death of his father, Pope Alexander, Borgia’s destruction quickly followed suit. Machiavelli would likely respond in one of two ways. Firstly, although Borgia was undoubtedly virtuous, he lacked sufficient Machiavellian virtue, evidenced by his not being named an armed prophet. While Machiavelli laments Alexander’s premature death, he also labels Borgia’s decision not to veto the appointment of Julius II a “mistake” on multiple occasions (P 7). Borgia clearly could have instituted a pope who would have obeyed him out of fear, or at least vetoed those with whom he had had conflicts in the past. If he were more skillful and virtuous, he would not have experienced such shortsightedness in the matter of papal succession and, consequently, not be led to his demise by it. 

Secondly, the nature of how Borgia relied on fortune for his first opportunity was fundamentally different from how the armed prophets did. Borgia’s power was derived from the good fortune of another, making it unstable and easily undercut. By contrast, the armed prophets depended on fortune simply for the opportunity to express their virtue. The two are clearly different: although Machiavelli does not care much, if at all, for moral virtue in the contemporary sense, it still feels egregious to ascribe finding a people group suffering (as the armed prophets did) to good fortune. Thus, despite being nominally the same, there is clearly a difference of kind between the fortune that Borgia and the armed prophets founded their regimes on. A more nuanced view of fortune’s political influence, then, may be that fortune as a means to create opportunities can serve as the groundwork from which a lasting regime can be built. But, good fortune given by another is not stable enough to support such an enterprise, even for relatively virtuous princes.

Overall, Machiavelli views fortune as an unpredictable force that is outside human control. Nevertheless, through exercising sufficient Machiavellian virtue, one can protect oneself and one’s nation against the harmful effects brought on by fortune’s whims. Yet, despite his aversion to depending on fortune, Machiavelli recognizes its initial necessity in political life. If luck is simply a catalyst for displaying one’s virtue and does not form the backbone of one’s success, he would likely approve of its presence in founding a new state. Thus, it is apparent that Machiavelli’s political philosophy is not a fixed doctrine, but rather a general guideline that is consistently amended with qualifications and caveats. Indeed, his discussion of fortune is wrought with intricacies and inconsistencies that leave much room for debate and misinterpretation. While these complexities cannot be easily and satisfactorily reconciled, perhaps they are representative of fortune’s unpredictability, a quality that renders it extremely difficult to pinpoint.
