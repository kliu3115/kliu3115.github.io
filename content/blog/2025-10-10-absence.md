---
title: "Artful Absence: How Non-Participants Shape Plato and Aristotle’s Poleis"
date: 2025-10-10
blurb: A piece that contrasts Plato and Aristotle's political philosophies through their attitudes towards non-participants. 
---
To justify justice for its own benefit and define a “good life” marked by eudaimonia or happiness, Plato in The Republic and Aristotle in Politics, respectively, construct ideal cities to understand the soul’s function and investigate the conditions necessary for human flourishing. Both explorations detail social hierarchies, spell out educational regimes and grapple with the tension between citizens and different forms of government. However, though the treatment of a city’s constituents reveals much about its purpose, much can be gleaned from investigating the people who are excluded. By asking questions about why certain groups are absent, whether such absence was voluntary, and how one’s absence impacts the other members of the city, each philosopher’s unique take on human nature and political order is illuminated. These "exceptional cases" of exclusion highlight an important contrast: while Plato is pessimistic about the average citizen’s rational capacity and insists on using strict control to maintain justice, Aristotle's more sympathetic view and recognition of natural exceptions creates a city that cultivates virtue in those capable of achieving it. 

In Plato’s view, the average citizen (a non-philospher) is ill-equipped to make good judgements and resist the emotions elicited by brilliant storytelling. Therefore, they must be fed a single, simple narrative to maintain political unity. To emphasize the non-philosopher’s poor judgement, he explains that even those who are naturally gifted by birth can be swayed by corrupting influences from a young age. Plato argues that the circumstances that most alleged philosophers are raised under — being “[presented] with…prayers and plaudits” for their “disposition to learn, good memory, courage and greatness of spirit” — leave them with “a very exalted idea of [themselves]” (494b-d). In other words, philosophical nature alone is insufficient to guarantee good judgement, as societal influences play a large part in clouding one’s judgement. Because even those who are born with a philosophical nature can be corrupted by the allure of flattery and bribery, he concludes that “it’s impossible for the masses to be philosophical” (494a). After all, they are not only subject to society’s corruption, but also lack the innate rationality to resist it. 

Moreover, Plato’s definition of the philosophical disposition further emphasizes the dangers of imitation. To him, being philosophical means possessing “knowledge which makes decisions about the city as a whole,” being “sound in judgement, and truly wise” (428c-e). Since the masses are not philosophical, they are incapable of self-governance and analyzing competing claims. Thus, the man who can “imitate anything” must be sent away, as his flowery language will not only conflict with “the decent man’s way of speaking,” but also with the “patterns…laid down at the beginning of [Plato’s] attempt to provide an education for [his people]” (398a-b). Plato’s city is united under a carefully constructed narrative (e.g. the Noble Lie he hopes to propagate) and imitation introduces threats to that unity.  In order to prevent the subversion of his hypothetical regime, Plato elects to censor the exceptionally talented imitator who could undermine the stories and lies that the city is built on and lead the unenlightened citizens astray. 

The aforementioned misguidance of individuals is especially problematic for Plato, who believes the individual and city are analogous in structure and nature. In fact, his construction of the just city to define justice in the soul hinges on the fact that there is a “likeness of the [city] in the form the [individual] takes” (369a). The soul and the city mirror one another and, if individuals deviate from reason and become subject to the whims of appetite and spirit, so too will their city. Combined with the masses' want of mental fortitude, it is clear that restraining freedom of speech, and by extension freedom of thought, is directly correlated with the city remaining just and rationally ordered. Because of this, Plato rebukes the role of poets, arguing that they “corrupt even good people” with their colorful imitations that persuade the people to “surrender [themselves] to [emotion]” (605c-d). Since poetry appeals to the non-rational parts of the soul, it undermines reason’s influence, allowing the spirit and appetite to take over. By extension from the individual to the city, if the general populace is overcome by emotion, then “pleasure and pain will be twin kings in [their] city in place of…reason” (607a). In order to keep his polis stable, Plato must control the non-philosopher class with carefully curated stories. This way, he can propagate a uniform political narrative by essentially doing the thinking for the masses, who he believes is incapable of reason and too easily enticed by emotion. 

The same logic that pushes Plato to banish the imitative poet underscores his description of justice, as political order depends on each person accepting their role rather than being misled by unruly desires. Plato’s city aims to illuminate the definition of justice by examining it at a larger scale. Just as he expelled the imitator who causes disharmony by appealing to the non-rational parts of the soul, so too does he expel that which disrupts his division of the city. In his words, the “greatest harm to the city” is “the interference [and interchange] of the three classes with one another” (434c). Justice, for him, is “the ability of the commercial, auxiliary and guardian classes to mind their own business, with each of them performing its own function in the city” (434d). Taken together with his strong rebuke of the poets, it is clear that Plato’s argument heavily emphasizes the need for restraint. Since people cannot be trusted to think rationally, he resorts to censorship and a rigid social structure to maintain civic order. 

In contrast, although Aristotle generally views human nature in a more positive light, he also concedes that there is a small population who match Plato’s description of the unwise general public. In the opening chapters of Politics, Aristotle explicitly highlights two groups that do not take part in his community: those who are “incapable of sharing” (“beasts”) and those who are “in need of nothing through being self-sufficient” (“gods”) (1253a27-30). Aristotle’s description of a “beast” strongly matches the description Plato gives of almost everyone in his polis. Interestingly, Aristotle, himself, does not explicitly remove the “beasts” (nor the “gods,” for that matter); rather, he insinuates that by nature, they are “no part of a city” (1253a28). The “beasts” cannot engage in rationally sound interaction with other citizens and therefore do not gain the benefits that participation in a city must have. After all, the end goal, or telos, of a city is to facilitate the “good life” of its citizens. If one does not come closer to virtue or happiness by participating in a city, then they are not truly part of one. Another key difference between Plato and Aristotle is that, for Aristotle the “beasts” constitute a minority and therefore can be discounted without significantly undercutting the population. To him, the general public has “by nature an impulse toward this sort of community” and possesses a baseline capacity for rational thought (1253a30-1). In this way, Aristotle is able to maintain his argument that a city must bring its constituents closer to a virtuous life, while also justifying a more sympathetic view of people’s rational capacity. 

Consequently, it is precisely because the ability to reason is not limited to a ruling class in Aristotle’s community that he does not feel the need to eliminate the exceptionally enlightened or virtuous people. While he recognizes that the average person does not possess well-rounded rationality, he believes that such people can still recognize virtue. When discussing the dangers of ostracism, he states that “surely no one would assert that [someone outstanding on the basis of virtue] should be expelled or banished” nor that “there should be rule over such a person” (1284b28-30). The exceptionally virtuous person would become a king, rather than being kicked out as in Plato’s city. This is in line with Aristotle’s creation of a polis that makes its citizens more virtuous, as the average person would undoubtedly benefit from being ruled by someone who is the epitome of virtue. Yet, Aristotle also insinuates that, on their own volition, such exceptionally virtuous people, or “gods,” would leave because they are neither gaining happiness nor virtue from partaking in the polis. As Aristotle puts it, the city “exists for the sake of living well” and active participation in politics yields benefits (1252b30, 1325b14-16). For the one who is already self-sufficient, the city has nothing more to provide, no way to make their lives more virtuous or happier, so they would necessarily leave. Thus, having seen why neither the “beasts” nor the “gods” participate in the city, it becomes clear that Aristotle’s polis exists for those with potential to achieve happiness through socio-political interaction. 

By comparing the rationale behind why certain people are not present in Plato and Aristotle’s cities, the differences in their ideologies become apparent. For Plato, the imitative poet poses a serious threat to the rational order of the soul and city because of his corrupting influence on the delicate balance of their tripartition. In removing the poet, Plato ensures that justice (in the sense that everyone adheres to their own roles) is upheld. In contrast, Aristotle’s city does not kick people out, but rather, argues that those who are incapable of benefiting from their interactions with the polis naturally exist outside it. Together, these “exceptional cases” highlight a key distinction in each philosopher’s goals for city building: Plato seeks to uphold justice by keeping people safe from their own irrationality, while Aristotle seeks to cultivate virtue and happiness for his constituents.
