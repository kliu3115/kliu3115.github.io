---
title: "Agency and Anacyclosis in Polybius’ Histories: The Role of Individuals and Nations"
date: 2025-11-24
blurb: A piece that highlights Polybius' cyclical theory of history. 
---

Polybius differentiates his Histories from that of other ancient historians by the scope of his work. While other historians focused on isolated events, Polybius presents a universal history that spans both time and space. He argues that this approach enables him to compare “parallel occurrences” and “arrive at [a] far more reliable general picture than is possible if everything is judged in isolation” (3.32). One of the chief generalizations Polybius presents is his theory of anacyclosis, which details an inevitable cycle of constitutions that every state follows: monarchy, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mobocracy, and back to monarchy. Yet, while anacyclosis may initially seem deterministic, Polybius’ vision is anything but. In fact, his vision leaves considerable room for human agency. He attributes the decline of a state to both internal and external sources, writing that while "internal evolution…pursues a regular sequence,” external influences are not bound by “any fixed principle,” and therefore highly variable (6.57). Through his parallel discussions of Scipio and Hannibal, as well as Rome and Carthage, Polybius demonstrates that, although history broadly follows predictable cycles, the character of individuals and nations can alter when and how the cycle unfolds. 

Known for his foresight and rationality, Scipio delayed the process of anacyclosis for the Roman Republic through his battlefield prowess and the tactical, calculating nature of his character. While other historians attribute Scipio’s success to fortune’s favor, Polybius insists that it stemmed from keen judgment, not divine accident. For example, Scipio’s judicious recognition that his men would not “venture upon great risks without the hope of some divine aid” led him to foster “their readiness to face dangerous enterprises by instilling into them the faith that his plans were divinely inspired” (10.2). Polybius stresses that, despite what he convinced his men of, Scipio’s plan was not the product of divine sanction, but rather his own “calculation and foresight” (10.2). By pinpointing the worries of his men, Scipio was uniquely able to address their concerns, inspiring courage in place of fear. In this way, he delayed Rome’s decline by emboldening his troops to fight more valiantly for their country. Moreover, Polybius reaffirms Scipio’s character through the testimonies of Scipio’s close associates who describe him as “astute and discreet and [possessing] a mind which was always concentrated upon the purpose he had in view” (10.3). More concretely, in battle, his strategic displays of bravery and calculated restraint undeniably steered battles in Rome’s favor. When his father was “surrounded by the enemy [and] dangerously wounded,” Scipio “charged by himself with reckless daring against the encircling cavalry,” an action which impelled his comrades to attack as well (10.3). Yet, this display of bravery does not purport Scipio as someone who trusts luck blindly. Polybius notes that, after having “won…a reputation for bravery,” Scipio “[refrained] from exposing himself to danger when his country’s entire hopes rested upon his safety” (10.3). By taking action strategically, Scipio is able to temporarily buffer Rome against the cyclical rise and fall of states and demonstrate that human agency can redirect the momentum of anacyclosis without escaping it entirely. 

In contrast, Hannibal exemplifies how individual agency can accelerate the process of anacyclosis through his bold leadership and emotional intelligence, which threatened the stability of the Roman Republic. First and foremost, Polybius highlights Hannibal’s leadership through his ability to “impose the authority of…a single will even upon men of totally diverse origins,” often in opposition to the “gale of fortune” (10.19). In particular, his acute understanding of his army’s diverse composition and each demographic group’s inclinations allowed him to prevent internal fragmentation and consolidate power effectively. For example, to regulate the impatience and “fickleness of the Celts,” Hannibal chose to “resume his advance as quickly as possible and so satisfy the expectations of his troops” (3.78). Rather than let the eclectic nature of his army weaken him, Hannibal’s influential personality unified them into a force that could threaten Rome’s stability and the pace of its history. Beyond his charisma, Hannibal’s emotional intelligence allowed him to accelerate the anacyclosis of Rome by manipulating its allies. Instead of demanding ransom from captured Italians, he released them without payment in hopes of “[alienating] them from their attachment to Rome…and [stirring] up revolt among those who believed that their harbours or cities had suffered decline as a result of Roman rule” (3.77). Polybius presents these choices that oppose expectation and common practice as deliberate and unmistakably rooted in Hannibal’s character; they could not be the result of divine or random forces. In doing so, Hannibal accelerates anacyclosis in the Roman Republic and shows how extraordinary individuals can quicken the pace of history without altering its general trajectory. 

Despite Hannibal’s personal strength of character, the broader character of Carthage limits his influence. Carthage’s lack of self-sufficiency and avarice as a nation made the state especially vulnerable to the degeneration dictated by anacyclosis. Although Carthage had a strong, well-trained navy, they also “largely [neglected] their infantry,” opting to supplement this deficiency by “[employing] foreign and mercenary troops” (6.52). Such dependence on mercenaries, who fought for monetary gain rather than civic duty or loyalty to their homeland, understandably weakened the Carthaginian army. Thus, it is unsurprising that, even with a capable commander like Hannibal, Carthage still struggled to maintain their military success: national character constrains individual influence. One might argue that the conflict between Carthage and Rome is inherently unfair because, in being an older state than Rome, Carthage would naturally be further along the path of decline. Therefore, it would be fallacious to compare a Carthage whose “strength had begun to decline” to “Rome…at its height” (6.51). In his arguments, Polybius recognizes this fact and even praises Carthage for being “well-designed at the outset in its most important features” and for being a mixed government like that of Rome (6.51). However, he also notes that key differences in the two states’ moral character make Carthage more susceptible and Rome more resistant to decline. Notably, Carthage’s reliance on mercenaries underscores the value its society places on money. In Carthage, bribery was “openly practiced by candidates for office,” a practice which demonstrates the great extent to which material gain outweighed civic duty for them. Because public benefit is subordinate to personal profit, Carthage’s political culture fosters the very vulnerabilities that hasten its decline. Thus, it is unsurprising that, in a state in which its leaders are not loyal to its constituents, the cycle of anacyclosis would naturally be accelerated.

Conversely, the patriotic and religious character of Rome sets it apart from its contemporaries, enhancing its resistance to anacyclosis. In direct contrast with Carthage, the Romans relied primarily on “the bravery of their own citizens” who would “carry on the war with all their resources,” even if they “happen to be defeated at the outset” (6.51). The differences in the two armies’ battlefield resolve cannot be understated. While the Romans fought for their homeland, the Carthaginian mercenaries did so for monetary gain. The Carthaginian commitment to avarice would have been inconceivable to the Romans, for whom there is “nothing more disgraceful than to receive bribes or seek gain by improper means” (6.56). The Roman laws and customs around war and money emphasize the maintenance of justice, which in turn helps the state resist the cyclical decline driven by fortune. Additionally, Rome’s adoption of a unified religious belief also prevents their downfall by reinforcing societal cohesion. As Polybius puts it, because the masses are “always fickle,” they must be restrained by the “mysterious terrors” that religious stories often entail (6.56). By curtailing the lawless desires of the common people, Rome exercises control over the unenlightened general public and ensures unity within the state. Together, these civic virtues of patriotism and religious adherence enable Rome to maintain internal stability and delay the cyclical decline that befell other states. 

As in the cases of Scipio and Hannibal who each used a combination of prudence, bravery and leadership to affect the course of history, exceptional individuals and their decisions are eclipsed by the civic character and broader social structures that govern states. Despite Hannibal’s bold leadership and emotional aptitude, in the long term, he was ultimately unable to overcome Carthage’s cupidity and dependence on other states. Similarly, Scipio’s tactful nature complemented the moral integrity of Rome but did not replace it: it was still its overall character and disciplined, patriotic citizenry that prolonged the Roman Republic’s success. However, even great empires are subject to the whims of fortune, which, for Polybius, impels history’s cyclical nature. Thus, while national character may trump the influence of individual personalities, both are only able to hasten or delay the next stage of anacyclosis, never completely avoiding it.
