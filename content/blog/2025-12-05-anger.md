---
title: "Anger’s Role in the Good Life: An Evaluation of Seneca’s Arguments for its Complete Absence"
date: 2025-12-5
blurb: A piece debating the validity of the Stoic view of anger, and emotion at large. 
---

In the Preface of Seneca’s On Anger, he lists a variety of anger’s harmful effects and illustrates its pervasive and destructive nature. To Seneca, anger is “the most hideous and frenzied of all the emotions,” “oblivious of decency” and “incapable of discerning [the] truth,” among other terrible qualities (1.1). It sticks out in both animals and people alike, driving them to madness, aggression and even murder and massacre in the worst cases. However, while observations and descriptions of anger’s detriment are valuable, Seneca, being a philosopher, spends much of his work using logic and reasoning to prove that anger should have no place in human life. By working through the definition of anger and how it arises and dismantling various arguments for its utility, Seneca arrives at the conclusion that a life marked by apatheia, or devoid of affections, is the best. 

In order to reach a conclusion about what anger’s role in human life should be, Seneca must first define anger. To arrive at the Stoic view, Seneca aggregates and qualifies four definitions from various named and unnamed philosophers. The first three definitions he invokes are placed in tandem with one another: anger is a) “a burning desire to avenge a wrong” b) “a burning desire to punish him by whom you think yourself to have been unfairly harmed” and c) “an incitement of the mind to damage him who has done damage or wished to do damage” (1.2.3). Taken together, it becomes apparent that being wronged, both in actuality and potentiality, is sufficient to incite anger. The former is more obvious: if I have been dropkicked, I have clearly been wronged and damaged. 

However, Seneca goes further, claiming that “those who are about to harm us” have already done so because “the very intention is what does harm” and “anyone on the point of doing a wrong is already doing one” (1.3.1). For example, if someone planned to dropkick me but never did it because they were restrained by the police, their intention to wrong me had been acted upon, despite it being cut short by external forces. Thus, I am still justified in being angry and feeling wronged by their ill intention. Essentially, he argues that one’s anger must be justified by moral culpability, which may or may not equate to material harm. Indeed, for Seneca, if moral culpability was lacking, then one’s reaction would be “quasi-anger” not real anger, because they have not actually been harmed by any intention to do wrong. For instance, if my brother dropkicked me purely by accident and I became angry at him as a result, that would be quasi-anger as he had no intention of harming me, but was rather just playing his part in the Logos’ best possible world. Thus, a necessary condition for anger is being wronged intentionally. 

But, Seneca notes that this alone is insufficient for anger to truly overwhelm one’s being. The earlier definitions, alongside the final one he invokes — Aristotle’s definition of anger from De Anima as “‘a burning desire to avenge a wrong’” — show that anger requires the mind to assent to the impression of being wronged and desire punishment as a result (1.2.3). Here, he introduces an interesting duality: while “anger may be the enemy of reason, it cannot, all the same, come into being except where there is a place for reason.” (1.3.4). Despite not being able to rein in anger once it is let loose, reason is a necessary condition for anger. This is why animals are unable to be truly angry because they lack the rationality characteristic of humans; therefore, anything they do that could be perceived as anger is really just an “impulse to fits of frenzy, ferocity and aggression” (1.3.4). Thus, Seneca would likely define anger as a desire to inflict punishment on those one has intentionally been wronged by. 

Having defined anger as such, Seneca now moves onto his chief task of deducing what anger’s role in human life should be. To start, he examines the question of whether or not anger is natural; after all, if anger is part of human nature, then it necessarily will have a place in human life. From the gradual regimes that doctors and governments alike prescribe to cure people of ailments and undesirable characteristics, Seneca concludes that “the nature of man…is not eager to punish” (1.6.4). For instance, if my brother chronically dropkicked people, a doctor would employ a series of moderate chastisements to try and cure him (i.e. changing his diet, recommending other ways of expressing his emotions, getting him counseling, etc.), before chopping his leg off entirely. In fact, such an extreme change would only be affected if “its effects are wholesome” and would not be seen as a punishment, but rather a benefit (1.6.2). Conversely, Seneca describes anger as “greedy [and eager] for punishment” (1.5.3, 6.4). Indeed, Seneca views man and anger as polar opposites: while man is mild, “loving of others,” “begotten for mutual assistance” and “seeks to help,” anger is cruel, “adverse,” begotten for mutual destruction and seeks to harm (1.5.2). Thus, being so at odds, the two cannot be in accordance with one another, therefore, anger is not natural. 

But, even if anger is not natural, if it can be controlled and used, then it can still play a role in human life by positively influencing it towards virtue and eudaimonia. This begs the question of the power reason and anger hold with respect to one another: is anger stronger or weaker than reason? While Seneca doesn’t provide an explicit answer, it can be shown that, in either case, anger is not both controllable and useful. To start, assume anger was stronger than reason, then “how can reason put a limit on it?” (1.8.5). In this case, Seneca would say that anger is “as useless as a subordinate in the soul as a soldier who ignores the signal for retreat” and would only wreak havoc on reason’s plans (1.9.2). It follows clearly that anger would not be controllable by reason and uncontrollable things should be precluded because “it is easier to exclude than to moderate them afterwards” (1.7.2). Thus, if anger is stronger than reason, it cannot be controlled in a meaningful or rational way and should be excluded from human life. 

On the other hand, assume anger was weaker than reason. Then, “reason can do without it” because “it is sufficient by itself for getting things done and has no need for a weaker ally” (1.8.5). In the Stoic view, rationality is what orders the cosmos and, to live the best possible life, one ought to follow reason. If both reason and anger were given simultaneous control over one’s life, then it would be worse than the life dictated purely by rationality. Anger would constantly be in conflict with reason’s decisions and, in the cases that the two agree, it would likely be quicker and easier to have reason alone in charge. As in the case of a bureaucracy, even if all parties agree, needing the approval of more people is necessarily less efficient. Therefore, anger is not useful if it is weaker than reason, and we have shown that, regardless of whether anger is stronger or weaker than reason, it is not both controllable and useful and should have no place in human life.
Finally, Seneca cements anger’s absence from life by dismantling the view that it serves purpose by facilitating punishments. Building on his earlier arguments, he revisits the idea of chastisement as a corrective, saying that “punishment serves the more to improve if it is imposed with considered judgement” (1.15.3). Reason is the “good judge [that] condemns what is damnable, [but] does not hate it” because, like the law, reason is impartial and constant (1.16.6). By contrast, anger is fickle, has “no solid strength” and results in “equal crimes [receiving] unequal punishments” (1.17.4, 7). For example, driven by anger, a store owner might kill the first two people he sees in a band of robbers, but, overcome with pity at their pleas for mercy, let the others leave unscathed. This is clearly unfair and demonstrates the capriciousness of anger, and affections in general, that make it unsuitable for effectively and justly administering punishments. In summary, on top of being against human nature and unable to be simultaneously controlled and useful, anger also fails as an overseer of punishment, further supporting its exclusion from the virtuous human life.  

However, many of Seneca’s arguments can be disrupted by Plato and Aristotle’s concepts of anger and the soul. Firstly, a chief objection Plato might raise against Seneca is that Seneca’s concept of the soul is too simple, which leads to false conclusions about anger’s utility. In Plato’s tripartite model of the soul, the rational part desires truth, the spirited part desires honor and the appetitive part desires physical and material pleasures. Although they may be at odds with one another at times, this makes them no less natural parts of the soul. Moreover, while Plato agrees that the rational element should rule because it is wise, he also states that the spirited part should “perform his own proper task,” which is “to be subordinate [and] the ally of the rational element” (Plato, Republic, 4.441e). More concretely, in line with his soul-city analogy, while the auxiliaries are undoubtedly subservient to the guardians, they are no less useful because they enforce the laws set forth by the guardians. Likewise, anger serves as a faithful sidekick that dutifully carries out reason’s wishes (in most cases). By this logic, it is possible for anger to be weaker than reason, and thus subject to its control, while still being useful. 

Additionally, although Seneca invokes Aristotle’s definition of anger and states that it “is not far from [his],” the two differ more than they initially seem (1.2.3). Whereas Seneca believes anger is purely a rational misjudgment, Aristotle pioneers a hylomorphic model that describes anger as being both physical and psychological. For Aristotle, the form of anger is “a desire for retaliation” and the matter is “the boiling of the blood and hot stuff around the heart” (Aristotle,  De Anima, 1.1.403a31-5). Similar to Plato’s potential objections, Aristotle, too, could put pressure on the Stoic argument by saying that, in focusing solely on reason, their model of anger and the world in general is too simple and not representative of reality. A more interesting point of contention, though, is Aristotle’s support for moderation and his claim that the virtues are situated at the mean between two vices. Applying this spectrum to anger yields either an excess (irascibility), a deficiency (inirasciblity) or the mean between the two extremes (good temper) (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, 2.71108aa4-9). Aristotle’s good tempered man would be one who, guided by reason, has chosen the mean. After all, as Seneca would agree, an excess of anger cannot be good. But a complete absence of anger is also not ideal because it leaves one vulnerable and unable to protect themselves against immediate threats due to their extreme passivity. Thus, anger could have a place in human life in moderation, as Aristotle believes is possible, because of its utility in responding to actions that require immediate retaliation or punishment.

Despite these objections, the Stoic argument retains much of its strength and persuasiveness. Both Aristotle and Plato recognize the value of rationality in living a virtuous life and the importance of controlling one’s spirit or affections. Although it can be argued that the Stoic definition is too extreme, oversimplified and not an accurate model of the real world, taking the parameters to the extreme (i.e. maximizing reason and minimizing anger) lead to insightful conclusions about what one should emphasize to live the best life. If nothing else, it serves as a valuable thought experiment that prompts people to reflect on the extent to which affective impulses override rational judgment in their lives and the negative consequences that result.
